# ADDENDUM: DOMINATION AND EXPLOITATION

*The reciprocal relationships of emancipatory praxis*

To practise praxis is to confront the relationships through which domination and exploitation are constituted, reproduced, and transformed.

Domination and exploitation are not separate problems to be addressed in succession. Nor is exploitation an additional concern to be appended to an otherwise complete account of emancipation.

**They are distinct but reciprocally constituted relationships through which power, dependency, material conditions, and the possibilities for agency develop.**

Domination creates conditions under which exploitation becomes possible, normalised, and durable. Exploitation, in turn, creates and maintains the material and social conditions through which domination becomes possible, normalised, and durable.

Neither relationship can be reduced to the other. Neither must precede the other. Their particular forms and interactions emerge historically, through the practices by which people organise their relationships with one another and with nature.

This reciprocity has profound implications for emancipatory praxis.

We cannot adequately challenge domination without examining the exploitation it enables, sustains, or conceals. Nor can we adequately challenge exploitation without examining the relationships of power through which it is organised, justified, and reproduced.

A democratic institution may preserve exploitative relationships. A redistribution of resources may preserve domination. A transformation that challenges one may inadvertently reproduce the other.

The task of emancipatory praxis is therefore not simply to oppose domination and exploitation as two separate injustices.

It is to understand and transform **the reciprocal relationships through which each constitutes the conditions for the other's reproduction**.

And because these relationships are reproduced through practice, their transformation requires the praxis of practice itself.

We must examine not only who exercises power, but how that power shapes the appropriation of labour, resources, and ecological benefits. We must examine not only who benefits from collective activity, but how those benefits shape the distribution and exercise of power.

We must ask how domination enables exploitation, how exploitation sustains domination, and how their reciprocal operation constrains the development and exercise of agency.

Most importantly, we must ask how our own practices participate in these relationships, even when those practices are explicitly intended to be emancipatory.

**For emancipation cannot be understood as freedom from domination with exploitation left intact, nor as freedom from exploitation with domination left intact. It requires the transformation of the relationships through which both are reproduced.**
