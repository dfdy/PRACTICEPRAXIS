# PRACTICE PRAXIS!

*A polemic against the politics of waiting*

By **Diaan Mynhardt**

Read the [addendum: Domination and Exploitation](ADDENDUM.md).

We have become remarkably accomplished at discussing what ought to be done.

We convene. We consult. We deliberate. We develop frameworks, establish committees, draft principles, and formulate strategies. We speak of participation, empowerment, transformation, and emancipation. We produce increasingly sophisticated accounts of why the world must change.

And then we wait.

We wait for permission. For funding. For consensus. For leadership. For the right conditions. For someone with sufficient authority to tell us that we may begin.

Meanwhile, the world continues to be made and remade through the practices of everyday life. Hierarchies reproduce themselves. Dependencies deepen. Possibilities disappear. And we congratulate ourselves on having understood the problem.

**Understanding domination is not the same as challenging it.**

Praxis, the reciprocal relationship between reflection and action, has become a comfortable word. It allows us to describe our intellectual commitments as transformative without necessarily transforming anything. We reflect upon action, and act upon reflection, but too often our reflection becomes an institution unto itself.

We have mistaken the preparation for emancipation for emancipation itself.

## The politics of permission

Domination does not survive through coercion alone. It survives because its logic becomes ordinary.

We learn who may speak, who must listen, who may decide, and who must seek approval. We learn to recognise authority before we recognise possibility. We learn to mistake obedience for cooperation, conformity for solidarity, and silence for agreement.

Eventually, we cease to notice the permissions we are waiting for.

This is hierarchy's most enduring achievement: not merely restricting what people may do, but shaping what they imagine themselves capable of doing.

A person who has been repeatedly denied the opportunity to exercise judgement may come to doubt that judgement. A community whose decisions have always been made elsewhere may find collective self-government unfamiliar, even threatening.

And then the institutions responsible for this deprivation point to the resulting dependency as evidence that people are not yet ready to govern themselves.

What a magnificent circular argument!

We deny people opportunities to develop their capacities, then invoke their supposed lack of capacity to justify continued denial.

**The remedy is not another programme that teaches people to become ready for freedom. The remedy is to create the conditions in which freedom can be exercised.**

## Practice is not rehearsal

We are told that people must first be educated before they can participate. That they must acquire the necessary skills before they can take responsibility. That they must understand democracy before they can practise it.

But how does one learn to govern without governing?

How does one develop judgement without judging, cooperation without cooperating, initiative without initiating?

The answer is not to abandon education, expertise, or preparation. It is to reject the artificial separation between learning and doing.

We learn democracy by practising democracy. We learn solidarity through acts of solidarity. We develop our capacities for collective agency through the exercise of collective agency.

Not inevitably. Not without mistakes. Not without conflict.

But there is no emancipatory education that can substitute indefinitely for the exercise of freedom.

A commons is not constituted by declaring something common. It is constituted through the relationships by which people collectively sustain, govern, contest, and transform what they share.

The meeting matters. So does the meal. So does the person who notices what needs doing and begins. So does the person who refuses an unjust demand.

These are not merely preliminary activities before the real work of transformation.

**They are where transformation becomes possible.**

## The practice of praxis, the praxis of practice

Yet action alone is insufficient.

We can practise domination. We can become extraordinarily skilled at exploitation. We can reproduce hierarchy through the most democratic-looking procedures imaginable.

A meeting can have equal speaking time while preserving unequal power. A cooperative can distribute its profits fairly while exploiting those outside its membership. A participatory research project can invite everyone to contribute while reserving the authority to interpret their contributions for the researcher.

Practice does not become emancipatory simply because it is collective.

This is why we must go further than the familiar call to unite reflection and action.

We must make our practices themselves the objects of critical inquiry.

Not only: *What did we accomplish?*

But: *How did we accomplish it? Who could initiate? Who deferred? Who was excluded? Whose labour became invisible? Which relationships of power survived our supposed transformation?*

And, most importantly: *What must change in the way we practise if our next actions are to become more emancipatory than our last?*

This is the **praxis of practice**.

We transform our circumstances through activity, while transforming the practices through which we act.

The distinction matters because the institutions of domination are remarkably capable of surviving changes in personnel, policies, and vocabulary. We can replace the leader while preserving the authority of leadership. We can democratise a procedure while leaving its underlying assumptions untouched.

If everything changes except the relationships through which domination is reproduced, what, precisely, have we transformed?

## Refusal is an action

The demand to act must itself be challenged.

Not every refusal is passivity. Not every silence is submission. Not every withdrawal is defeat.

Sometimes the most consequential exercise of agency is the refusal to participate in a practice that demands one's subordination.

Refusing to legitimise an unjust procedure is an action. Declining to perform gratitude for paternalistic assistance is an action. Withholding cooperation from an exploitative arrangement can be an action.

We must reject the convenient fiction that agency is measured by visible activity, productivity, or willingness to participate.

A politics that demands constant participation can become another instrument of domination.

Emancipation must include the freedom to rest, to dissent, to withdraw, and to refuse.

And these freedoms must be substantive, not merely formal. The right to refuse means little when refusal costs a person their livelihood, security, or belonging.

**Agency is not simply the capacity to choose. It is constituted and exercised within the relationships that make choices possible, meaningful, or impossible.**

## Against emancipation by experts

There is a particular arrogance in the conviction that we must emancipate others.

It is often expressed with the best intentions.

We will empower them. We will give them a voice. We will build their capacity. We will teach them to participate.

Who, exactly, appointed us the custodians of their agency?

People are not empty vessels awaiting the gift of freedom. Nor does acknowledging structurally produced trauma, dependency, or constrained agency entitle us to diagnose entire communities as incapable.

The problem is not that some people possess agency and others lack it.

The problem is that social relationships distribute the conditions for exercising and developing agency profoundly unequally.

The task, therefore, is not to transfer agency from the powerful to the powerless as though it were a commodity.

It is to transform the relationships through which powerlessness is produced and maintained.

This requires resources. Land. Time. Education. Security. Accessible institutions. It requires confronting colonialism, patriarchy, class domination, and the exploitation of nature.

No amount of enthusiastic participation will compensate for the absence of material conditions.

But neither will material redistribution alone guarantee emancipation if the relationships through which people organise their lives remain unchanged.

## Begin, and remain answerable

We cannot know in advance everything that an emancipatory practice must become.

Indeed, any programme claiming to possess the final form of emancipation has already begun to betray it.

Our theories must be exposed to the world. Our principles must encounter the people whose lives they concern. Our practices must remain open to criticism, contradiction, and revision.

We must be willing to discover that the things we believed were emancipatory have reproduced domination.

And when that happens, we must resist the temptation to protect our theories from the evidence of our practices.

The contradiction is not an embarrassment to be concealed. It is an invitation to discover what our understanding has failed to recognise.

We must be prepared to change not only what we do, but what we believe we are doing.

This is neither activism without reflection nor reflection without action.

It is the refusal to grant either theory or practice immunity from transformation.

We do not need to wait until we have perfected the theory of democratic life before beginning to live democratically. Nor may we claim that our actions are democratic merely because we intended them to be.

We begin where we are, with the people and possibilities before us. We organise. We cooperate. We disagree. We make mistakes. We confront the consequences. We change our practices.

And we begin again, under conditions that our previous actions have helped to transform.

**Practise what you preach. But more importantly, question what your practice preaches.**

Practise solidarity without demanding conformity.

Practise democracy without surrendering dissent.

Practise cooperation without concealing conflict.

Practise emancipation without appointing yourself the emancipator.

Practise the freedom to refuse.

And refuse to treat any practice, including this one, as beyond criticism.

**Practice praxis!**

Not tomorrow, when the conditions are perfect.

Not after the committee has approved the possibility of initiative.

Not when everyone agrees.

Now.

And when your practice reproduces the very world you intended to transform, change the practice.

**For emancipation is not something we achieve before we can begin to live freely. It is something we struggle to constitute through the very practice of freedom.**
